\begin{abstract}
A glider and its shadow both appear in the Swiss national orthophoto mosaic
SWISSIMAGE. This note derives the glider's height above the ground from that
single image and explains each step. The basic shadow formula gives
\SI{142.5}{m}, but it is biased. An orthophoto draws an airborne object
where the camera ray through it meets the ground, not where the object is. We reconstruct
the exposure geometry from public flight metadata: a pushbroom camera with a \ang{45.3} field
of view flying at \SI{6393}{m}, \SI{481}{m} to the side of the glider. The orthophoto
therefore draws the glider about \SI{20}{m} too far north, almost straight towards its own shadow. We
also date the exposure to 11:25~UTC from the survey flight pattern and allow for the
glider's own motion during the scan. The geometry then closes, and the estimate becomes
\[
  h = \SI{180 \pm 3}{m}\ \text{above the terrain beneath the glider}
  \quad(95\,\%\text{ interval } \SIrange{173}{186}{m}).
\]
Two independent clocks agree: the shadow direction and the flight log date the
exposure to within 1.5~minutes of each other.
\end{abstract}

\section{The idea in one picture}

Sunlight arrives as parallel rays at a known elevation angle $\alpha$. The glider
blocks one ray, and the shadow lands where that ray meets the hillside. If we know how far
apart the glider and its shadow are horizontally ($D$), the height follows from a
right-angled triangle, $D\tan\alpha$. A correction is needed because the hillside is not
flat. That is all the shadow method is (Section~\ref{sec:shadow}).

The difficulty is that we never see the glider's true horizontal position. The
image is an \emph{orthophoto}, rectified so that everything \emph{on the ground}
is at its map position. A glider \SI{180}{m} above the ground is not on the ground. The camera
saw it slightly from the side, and the rectification projects it along the camera's line of sight onto the
terrain (Figure~\ref{fig:relief}). Here that slides the glider about
\SI{20}{m} towards its shadow, so the measured gap $D$ is too short. Section~\ref{sec:relief}
recovers the camera position and removes that error. This single correction moves the
answer from \SI{142}{m} to \SI{184}{m}. The remaining steps are refinements of a few metres.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Orthophoto.} SWISSIMAGE is swisstopo's national orthophoto mosaic. In this part of the
Alps it is built from \SI{0.25}{m} ground-sample-distance image strips. The covering strip is
\code{20230714\_1118\_12504}, flown on 14~July~2023. Its identifier encodes the
start of acquisition, 11:18~UTC. Its footprint polygon, length (\SI{57.87}{km}) and start point are public
(\code{data/imagery-acquisition.json}). The other eight strips flown that morning are also public
(\code{data/flight-strips-2023-07-14.json}).

\paragraph{Camera.} swisstopo flew a Leica ADS100 \emph{pushbroom} sensor. It does not take
frames. Each colour band is recorded by a single line of \num{20000} pixels across the flight
direction, and the image is built up line by line as the aircraft moves forward. The
orthophoto uses the \emph{nadir} line group, which looks straight down along-track. The camera's
SH120 head has a \SI{120}{mm} lens and a nadir field of view of \ang{45.2} across track. Its
flying-height multiplier is \num{24000}:1, so \SI{0.25}{m} pixels correspond to
\SI{6000}{m} above ground.

\paragraph{Terrain.} Elevations come from swissALTI3D, obtained via the geo.admin.ch height service.
Local grids around the glider (\SI{2.5}{m} spacing) and the shadow (\SI{2}{m}) are cached
in \code{data/terrain-grids.json} and bilinearly interpolated. The hillside under the glider
falls about \SI{30}{\degree} to the east and gently to the north.

\paragraph{Frames.} Horizontal coordinates are in CH1903+/LV95 (EPSG:2056).
Combining grid scale and height reduction, LV95 coordinate differences equal ground distances
to within $3\times10^{-4}$, i.e.\ \SI{3}{cm} per \SI{100}{m}. Only height \emph{differences} enter the
results, so the vertical datum cancels.

\section{Measuring the image}\label{sec:image}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig-measurement.jpg}
\caption{Left: the glider, \SI{40}{m} square, with its silhouette centroid (cross).
Centre: the shadow area \SI{76}{m} north-north-west, contrast-stretched. Right: the glider
silhouette, blurred by the solar disk, matched to the shadow (red outline). The tail and fuselage land on
the darkest blob, and the north-west wing lies along the dark band.}
\label{fig:measure}
\end{figure}

Both crops are fetched from the swisstopo WMS at \SI{5}{cm} per pixel, upsampled from the
\SI{25}{cm} source (\code{analysis/measure\_image.py}).

\paragraph{Glider.} The glider is the only large white object, and thresholding brightness
and saturation isolates its silhouette. The centroid is at
$(E',N') = (\num{2582425.79}, \num{1135135.40})$, within \SI{0.5}{m} of the original
hand-placed pin. The silhouette spans \SI{17.8}{m}, consistent with an 18-metre-class glider.
The T-tail is to the north-east, so the glider was heading about \ang{227} (south-west).

\paragraph{Shadow.} The shadow is faint, and that is expected.
The Sun's disk subtends \SI{9.3}{mrad}. From the glider--shadow distance along the ray, $(Z-z_s)/\sin\alpha\approx\SI{220}{m}$,
every edge is therefore smeared over about \SI{2}{m}, more than twice the chord of the wing (about
\SI{0.7}{m}). No point on the ground is fully shaded by the wing, so its shadow is a half-tone smudge. The
wider fuselage and tail make the darkest blob. To locate it objectively, we
\begin{enumerate}
  \item turn the glider silhouette into a \emph{predicted} shadow: invert it and convolve it
        with a disk of the penumbra width;
  \item high-pass filter the shadow crop (subtract a \SI{3}{m} Gaussian blur) to remove
        brightness gradients;
  \item cross-correlate the two over all shifts, scales 0.85--1.15 and stretches along the sun
        direction 1.0--1.35, which allow for slope foreshortening.
\end{enumerate}
The best match (Figure~\ref{fig:measure}, right) has scale 0.97 and no stretch. Its peak stands
5.8 standard deviations above the background correlation. It is stable for penumbra widths of
1--\SI{2}{m}, and it puts the shadow's centroid at $(E_s,N_s)=(\num{2582416.12},\num{1135210.68})$.
The correlation falls by one standard deviation within $\pm\SI{0.8}{m}$ along the sun direction.
Along the wing axis it falls more slowly, within $\pm\SI{2}{m}$. We carry
$\sigma=\SI{0.5}{m}$ and $\SI{1.2}{m}$ along those two directions.

Because the shadow is a parallel projection of the glider, the centroid of the shadow is the shadow of the
glider's centroid. Matching centroids therefore pairs corresponding points.

\paragraph{Result.} The image separation is $D' = \SI{75.89}{m}$ at azimuth $\ang{352.7}$, with terrain
$z_s = \SI{1905.9}{m}$ at the shadow and $z' = \SI{1927.5}{m}$ under the glider's image.

Over a few hundred metres, sunlight is a family of parallel rays with elevation $\alpha$ and
azimuth $A_\odot$. Earth curvature (\SI{1}{mm} over \SI{100}{m}) and refraction (below \ang{;;30}
at $\alpha\approx\ang{65}$) are negligible. A glider point at height $Z$ and horizontal position
$\mathbf g$ casts its shadow at $\mathbf s$ on terrain of height $z_s$, with
\begin{equation}
  Z - z_s = D\tan\alpha,\qquad D = \lVert\mathbf s-\mathbf g\rVert,\qquad
  \operatorname{az}(\mathbf s-\mathbf g) = A_\odot + \ang{180}.
  \label{eq:ray}
\end{equation}
The height above the ground directly beneath the glider is $h = Z - z_g$, so
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\,h = D\tan\alpha + z_s - z_g\,}
  \label{eq:height}
\end{equation}
Here the shadow falls about \SI{22}{m} downhill, so the correction $z_s-z_g$ is negative.
With the image positions taken at face value ($\mathbf g = (E',N')$) and $\alpha=\ang{65.19}$
(Section~\ref{sec:sun}), Equation~\eqref{eq:height} gives
$h = 75.89\times2.1625 + 1905.9 - 1927.5 = \SI{142.5}{m}$.
That is the \emph{naive} estimate. The next three sections correct its inputs.

\paragraph{Sensitivities.} $\partial h/\partial D = \tan\alpha = 2.16$ and
$\partial h/\partial\alpha = D\sec^2\alpha = \SI{7.5}{m}$ per degree.
Under a high summer sun, horizontal errors are amplified more than twofold, and a degree of solar error costs
several metres.

\section{Exposure time and solar position}\label{sec:sun}

\subsection{When was the glider photographed?}

The strip ID gives only the start time. However, the nine strips flown that morning form a
classic back-and-forth survey pattern, with alternate east- and westbound lines about \SI{2.2}{km} apart. The
interval between consecutive start times is the line's length divided by the ground speed, plus a
turn:
\begin{equation}
  t_{k+1} - t_k = \frac{L_k}{V_{\text{dir}(k)}} + T_{\text{turn}} .
\end{equation}
The start times are truncated to whole minutes. A least-squares fit with separate east- and
westbound speeds, Monte-Carlo'd over the sub-minute truncation, gives the following
(\code{analysis/fit\_camera.py}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item eastbound \SI{398}{km/h} (95\,\%: 326--508);
  \item westbound \SI{349}{km/h} (305--410);
  \item turn time \SI{4.2}{min}.
\end{itemize}
The difference between the two speeds is consistent with a wind. The glider lies \SI{44.13}{km} along the eastbound strip,
which gives the exposure at
\[
  t = \text{11:18} + (7.15\pm0.91)\ \text{min} = \text{11:25:09 UTC} \pm \SI{55}{s}.
\]

\subsection{Solar position}

The Sun's position follows Meeus' low-precision algorithm (as in the NOAA solar calculator,
accurate to about \ang{;1;}), implemented in \code{paper/solar.mjs}. In outline, the Sun's
apparent longitude $\lambda$ gives its declination, $\sin\delta = \sin\varepsilon\sin\lambda$.
The time, the equation of time and the longitude give the hour angle $H_\odot$. With latitude $\varphi$,
\begin{align}
  \sin\alpha &= \sin\varphi\sin\delta + \cos\varphi\cos\delta\cos H_\odot,\\
  A_\odot &= \operatorname{atan2}\!\bigl(\sin H_\odot,\;
             \cos H_\odot\sin\varphi - \tan\delta\cos\varphi\bigr) + \ang{180}.
\end{align}
At the glider ($\ang{46.3674}$\,N, $\ang{7.2103}$\,E), $\delta=\ang{21.68}$ and local
solar noon is at 11:37~UTC.

\ang{0.02}, i.e.\ \SI{0.2}{m} of height. The azimuth, in contrast, turns by \ang{0.55} per minute.
That makes the shadow direction a sensitive clock, which Section~\ref{sec:checks} uses as a check.

\subsection{Recovering the camera from the strip footprint}

A pushbroom line looks out to $\pm\beta$ on either side of the nadir track. Over a ridge the
swath edge meets the ground sooner, and over a valley later. A swath edge at terrain height $z$
lies at horizontal distance $(Z_c - z)\tan\beta$ from the nadir track, so the footprint width across
the track at any along-track position is
\begin{equation}
  w = (Z_c - z_N)\tan\beta + (Z_c - z_S)\tan\beta = \tan\beta\,\bigl(2Z_c - z_N - z_S\bigr),
  \label{eq:swath}
\end{equation}
where $z_N$ and $z_S$ are the terrain heights at the north and south edges. This is linear in the
unknowns $\tan\beta$ and $Z_c\tan\beta$. We measured $w$, $z_N$ and $z_S$ on 56 cross-sections of the
footprint polygon, one per kilometre. The strip crosses Lake Geneva (\SI{372}{m}) and Alpine ridges
above \SI{3000}{m}, which makes the fit well conditioned. The result is:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrl@{}}
\toprule
& fit (95\,\% bootstrap) & check \\
\midrule
field of view $2\beta$ & \ang{45.30} (\ang{45.12}--\ang{45.49}) & datasheet SH120 nadir: \ang{45.2} \\
flight altitude $Z_c$ & \SI{6393}{m} (6370--6417) & \num{24000}:1 $\times$ \SI{0.25}{m} = \SI{6000}{m} above $\approx$\SI{400}{m} \\
width residual & \SI{8.4}{m} RMS, $R^2 = 0.9997$ & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The fitted field of view matches the manufacturer's value to \ang{0.1}, and the altitude matches the nominal pixel
size over the lake. This confirms both the camera model and the constant-altitude assumption. Each
cross-section then gives the nadir track as the average of the two edge solutions,
$x_0 = \tfrac12\bigl[(N_{\text{edge}} - (Z_c-z_N)\tan\beta) + (S_{\text{edge}} + (Z_c - z_S)\tan\beta)\bigr]$.
The track is straight to \SI{7}{m} RMS. At the glider it passes $N = x_0 = \num{1134654.6}\pm\SI{1.3}{m}$, and we
carry $\pm\SI{4}{m}$ to cover local wander.

\subsection{The correction}

The glider's image lies $N' - x_0 = \SI{481}{m}$ north of the nadir track, and the camera is
$Z_c - z' = \SI{4466}{m}$ above the ground there. The ray reaches the ground at
$\tan\theta = 481/4466 = 0.1077$, i.e.\ $\theta = \ang{6.2}$ off vertical. By similar triangles, a point at
height $Z$ on that ray is drawn displaced away from the nadir by
\begin{equation}
  \Delta = (Z - z')\tan\theta \approx \SI{20}{m}\quad\text{(northwards)},
  \label{eq:relief}
\end{equation}
with the true position $N_g = x_0 + (N'-x_0)\,(Z_c - Z)/(Z_c - z')$. Because the pushbroom's nadir
lines look straight down along-track, there is no east--west displacement. The datasheet places the
nadir group at \ang{0}, and we allow $\pm\ang{0.5}$.

The sunlight runs \ang{6.6} west of north, almost exactly along $\Delta$. The true sun-ray distance
is therefore $D = D' + (Z - z')\tan\theta_\parallel$, with $\tan\theta_\parallel = \tan\theta\cos\ang{6.6} = 0.1070$.
Substituting into~\eqref{eq:ray} gives an equation that contains $Z$ on both sides. Solving it gives
\begin{equation}
  Z - z' = \frac{D'\tan\alpha + z_s - z'}{1 - \tan\theta_\parallel\tan\alpha}
         = \frac{164.1 - 21.6}{1 - 0.1070\times2.1625} = \frac{142.5}{0.769} = \SI{185.4}{m}.
  \label{eq:relief-solved}
\end{equation}
The denominator is the key quantity. Every metre the glider rises moves its image
\SI{0.107}{m} towards the shadow. That shortens the apparent baseline, and the shadow formula multiplies
the shortfall by $\tan\alpha$, so the bias feeds on itself. Here the feedback factor is $1/0.769 = 1.30$: the naive
estimate is \SI{30}{\percent} too low.

Finally, the ground must be read beneath the \emph{true} position, \SI{20}{m} south of the image,
where the terrain is \SI{1.3}{m} higher ($z_g = \SI{1928.8}{m}$). Then $h = \SI{184.1}{m}$.

\section{Glider motion during the scan}\label{sec:motion}

A pushbroom image is not a snapshot. The eastbound aircraft (about \SI{110}{m/s}) scans the shadow, which is
\SI{9.7}{m} further west, before the glider, so the two are recorded
$\Delta t = 9.7/110 \approx \SI{0.09}{s}$ apart. The glider is flying at, say, \SI{30}{m/s} on heading \ang{227}.
In that interval it moves \SI{2.6}{m} south-west, away from its shadow. The shadow in the image was
therefore cast from a point about \SI{2.6}{m} north-east of where the glider is drawn, which is closer to the
shadow. Allowing for this, with the general time offset
\begin{equation}
  \Delta t = \frac{E_g - E_s + (Z - z_s)\tan\gamma}{V},
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is the along-track look angle and $V$ the aircraft ground speed,
lowers the estimate to \SI{179.8}{m}. The glider's airspeed is not measured. We take
\SIrange{20}{40}{m/s} (72--\SI{144}{km/h}), the normal range for a glider, and heading
$\ang{227}\pm\ang{10}$ to allow for wind drift. The white glider shows no colour fringes, which confirms
that the colour bands of the nadir group were recorded simultaneously.

\section{The full model}\label{sec:model}

Collecting Sections~\ref{sec:shadow}--\ref{sec:motion}, the unknowns are the glider's true position
$\mathbf g=(E_g,N_g)$ and height $Z$ at the instant the scan reached it. The model is
\begin{align}
  E_g &= E' - (Z - z')\tan\gamma, &&\text{(camera ray, along track)}\\
  N_g &= x_0 + (N' - x_0)\,\frac{Z_c - Z}{Z_c - z'}, &&\text{(camera ray, across track)}\\
  (\mathbf s - \mathbf g + \mathbf v\,\Delta t)\cdot\hat{\mathbf u} &= \frac{Z - z_s}{\tan\alpha},
      &&\text{(sun ray)}
\end{align}
with $\hat{\mathbf u}$ the horizontal unit vector pointing away from the Sun and $\mathbf v$ the glider velocity.
For given inputs this is one equation in $Z$, solved by root-bracketing. The height above ground is then
$h = Z - z_{\text{DTM}}(\mathbf g)$ (\code{analysis/solve\_height.py}). The component of the sun-ray equation
\emph{across} $\hat{\mathbf u}$ is not used in the solution. It is kept as a residual that tests whether the
geometry closes.

\section{Consistency checks}\label{sec:checks}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Field of view and altitude.} These come from the footprint alone and match the
        manufacturer's datasheet (Section~\ref{sec:relief}).
  \item \textbf{Two clocks.} The shadow direction alone dates the exposure. After the relief
        correction, the shadow is exactly opposite the Sun at 11:26:35~UTC, which is
        $1.4\pm0.9$~min after the flight-log estimate of 11:25:09. Before the correction, the
        answer would have been 11:24. Both are well inside the survey window, and a shadow from another time of
        day would point tens of degrees away.
  \item \textbf{Closure.} At 11:25 the cross-ray residual is \SI{+1.3}{m} for a stationary
        glider and \SI{-0.9}{m} for one moving at \SI{30}{m/s}. Both are within the shadow's
        positional uncertainty. The geometry closes without any free parameter tuned to make it do so.
  \item \textbf{Shadow shape.} The matched template needs no stretch and a scale of 0.97. That is
        consistent with the glider image being enlarged across-track by $(Z_c - z')/(Z_c - Z) = 1.04$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Uncertainty}\label{sec:uncertainty}

\subsection{Monte Carlo}

All uncertain inputs are sampled together over \num{20000} draws:
\begin{itemize}
  \item exposure time $\mathcal N(7.15, 0.91)$~min after 11:18;
  \item glider centroid $\sigma=\SI{0.15}{m}$; shadow centroid $\sigma=\SI{1.2}{m}$ along the wing axis
        and \SI{0.5}{m} along the sun direction;
  \item $Z_c\ \mathcal N(6393, 12)$; $x_0\ \mathcal N(\text{fit}, 4)$; $\gamma\ \mathcal U(\pm\ang{0.5})$;
  \item glider speed $\mathcal U(20, 40)$~m/s; heading $\mathcal N(227, 10)$; aircraft $\mathcal N(110, 15)$~m/s;
  \item terrain: \SI{0.5}{m} DTM error at each end, a grass offset of 0 to \SI{0.5}{m} at the shadow, and
        \SI{1}{m} uncertainty in the surface the orthophoto was projected onto.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Worst-case intervals (calculator)}

The browser calculator in \code{glider-height/} implements Equation~\eqref{eq:height} without the camera
model. It uses exact worst-case intervals instead of Monte Carlo. $h$ increases with $D$,
$\tan\alpha$ and $z_s$, and decreases with $z_g$, so over an input box the extremes lie at corners:
\begin{align}
  h_{\min} &= (D-\varepsilon_D)\tan\alpha_{\min} + (z_s - z_g) - 2\varepsilon_z,\\
  h_{\max} &= (D+\varepsilon_D)\tan\alpha_{\max} + (z_s - z_g) + 2\varepsilon_z.
\end{align}
To reproduce the corrected result (\SI{179.8}{m}) in the calculator, enter the position from which the glider
cast the imaged shadow, i.e.\ corrected for relief displacement and for \SI{30}{m/s} of motion,
$(\num{2582427.72}, \num{1135117.71})$, with $z_g = \SI{1928.8}{m}$, the image-measured shadow
$(\num{2582416.12}, \num{1135210.68})$ with $z_s = \SI{1905.9}{m}$, and $\alpha = \ang{65.19}$.

\section{Alternative: reference masts}\label{sec:masts}

Instead of an ephemeris, $\alpha$ can be calibrated from a vertical mast of known height $H$, using
$\tan\alpha = (H + z_b - z_t)/L$, where $L$ is the mast's shadow length and $z_b$, $z_t$ the terrain
at its base and shadow tip. The calculator supports this, and two ski-lift masts nearby were
estimated at about \SI{9}{m} and \SI{10}{m} ($\pm\SI{3}{m}$) from ground photographs. At $\alpha\approx\ang{65}$,
however, a \SI{9}{m} mast casts only a \SI{4.2}{m} shadow, and $\partial h/\partial H = D/L \approx 18$. A
$\pm\SI{3}{m}$ uncertainty in the mast height would therefore spread the glider height over
$\pm\SI{55}{m}$. The ephemeris is about 300 times more precise here, so the masts are not used.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Shadow identity.} The shadow is identified by position, orientation, size and shape (it matches
        the glider's silhouette at scale 0.97 and the correct wing angle). It is faint, however, and no
        statistical interval covers a misidentification.
  \item \textbf{Mosaic seams.} The orthophoto is assumed to come from strip \code{20230714\_1118\_12504}.
        The strip covers the location, and the two clocks agree with it.
  \item \textbf{Glider attitude.} A banked glider has a different silhouette and shadow, but the matching is
        centroid-based and both silhouettes come from the same object.
  \item \textbf{Glider speed.} The speed is assumed, not measured. In principle it is encoded in the pushbroom
        distortion of the glider's image, which shears the wing--fuselage angle. Measuring that distortion
        could remove the second-largest error term.
\end{itemize}
